Commitment, Credibility, and Deterrence

When structure becomes strategy

Earlier chapters showed how structural commitments influence the parameters that determine equilibrium competition.

Cost structure, differentiation, and infrastructure affect the demand and cost conditions under which price competition occurs. Once those conditions are set, equilibrium prices, quantities, and profit follow.

But structural commitments do more than shape equilibrium.
They also communicate intentions to competitors.

Large investments, infrastructure scale, and cost positions often signal how aggressively a firm is willing or able to compete in the future.
When these commitments are credible, they influence the expectations of rivals.
Those expectations, in turn, shape strategic behavior before competition even begins.

Commitment Changes Expectations

A structural commitment is more than a plan.

It is an action that becomes difficult or costly to reverse.

Building production facilities, expanding distribution networks, or investing in specialized technology often involves large fixed costs. Once these investments are made, the firm has strong incentives to utilize them.

Competitors recognize this.
A firm with large installed capacity may be expected to supply aggressively rather than allow that capacity to remain idle. A firm with very low marginal cost may be expected to tolerate lower prices than rivals can sustain.

These expectations alter how competitors anticipate future price competition.

Commitments therefore influence behavior not only through their direct economic effects but also through the signals they send about future conduct.

Capacity and Entry Deterrence

One form of structural commitment involves capacity.

A firm may build infrastructure capable of producing more output than current demand requires. At first glance, this appears inefficient.

However, large capacity can change how potential entrants evaluate the market.

If existing firms possess substantial unused capacity, new entrants may expect aggressive price competition once they begin operating. Incumbents could expand output quickly, pushing prices downward.

Anticipating this possibility, a potential entrant may conclude that post-entry equilibrium profit would be low.

Entry may therefore appear unattractive even if demand initially seems large.

In this way, capacity commitments can influence the expectations of potential competitors.

Whether such deterrence succeeds depends on credibility. Competitors must believe that incumbents would actually use their capacity aggressively if entry occurred.

Credible Cost Advantage

Cost positioning can create similar effects.

A firm that achieves a durable cost advantage may be able to sustain lower prices while remaining profitable.

Competitors understand that price competition against such a firm is likely to be difficult. If margins compress, the low-cost firm can remain profitable while higher-cost rivals struggle.

The credibility of this advantage depends on the source of the cost difference.

Temporary discounts or promotional pricing are easily reversed and may not change competitor expectations. Structural cost advantages created by technology, scale, or supply chain design are harder to imitate and therefore more credible.

When rivals believe that a firm truly possesses lower cost, they may anticipate more aggressive price competition.

This expectation can influence both entry decisions and competitive behavior within the market.

Why Some Threats Fail

Not all strategic threats succeed.

A firm may claim that it will respond aggressively to competition. But if the threat lacks credibility, competitors may ignore it.

For a threat to influence behavior, it must align with the economic incentives created by the firm’s structure.

If a firm threatens to cut prices sharply but lacks the cost structure to sustain those prices, rivals may conclude that the threat is unlikely to be carried out.
Similarly, promises to maintain high prices may not be credible if firms have large unused capacity and strong incentives to expand output.

Credibility arises when a firm’s structural commitments make certain actions economically rational.
Competitors respond not to statements but to incentives.

Strategic Commitments and Industry Outcomes

Structural commitments affect more than individual firms.
They can also influence the overall intensity of competition within an industry.

Some commitments soften rivalry. Differentiation investments, for example, may reduce substitution between products and allow firms to sustain higher prices. Other commitments intensify rivalry.

If several firms invest heavily in capacity or cost reduction, the resulting price competition may become more severe. Margins fall as firms attempt to utilize their expanded capacity or exploit their lower costs.

In such cases, the industry may experience structural escalation without corresponding improvements in profitability.

The Possibility of Escalation

When competitors respond symmetrically to structural commitments, escalation can occur.

Suppose one firm expands capacity to strengthen its competitive position. Rivals may respond with their own capacity investments to avoid losing market share. The resulting industry structure may contain far more capacity than demand requires.
Price competition then becomes intense as firms attempt to utilize their infrastructure.

Similarly, widespread investments in cost reduction may compress prices across the industry, leaving margins little changed despite large investments.

In these situations, structural commitments increase the scale of competition but not necessarily the profitability of the market.

Strategic Commitment and Profit

The examples above illustrate an important principle.

Structural commitments influence profit through two channels.

First, they alter the parameters of equilibrium competition by changing cost or demand relationships.
Second, they influence competitor expectations and strategic responses.

These two effects interact.

A commitment that improves cost position may increase profit if rivals cannot imitate it. The same commitment may produce little benefit if competitors respond with similar investments.

Understanding these interactions is essential for evaluating strategic decisions before revenue exists.

Designing Credible Positions

Successful strategic commitments typically share two characteristics.

They alter the parameters governing equilibrium competition.
They create incentives that competitors recognize as credible.

A firm that invests in durable differentiation, unique technology, or efficient infrastructure may reshape the competitive environment in its favor.
But the durability of such advantages depends on how rivals respond.

If competitors can replicate the commitment easily, the advantage may disappear as the industry adjusts.

If the commitment is difficult to imitate, the resulting equilibrium may remain favorable for an extended period.

Deterrence Is Not Always Profitable

Deterrence strategies often appear attractive in theory.

Preventing entry or discouraging competition seems likely to preserve profit.

In practice, deterrence frequently involves large structural investments that may themselves reduce profitability.

Building excess capacity, maintaining large infrastructure, or investing heavily in cost reduction may deter entry, but these actions can also increase fixed costs or intensify price competition.

The net effect on profit depends on how competitors respond and how equilibrium adjusts.

Strategic commitments therefore require careful evaluation rather than simple assumptions about their effectiveness.

Commitment as a Strategic Choice

The framework developed in this book now extends from demand estimation to strategic commitment.

Firms begin by estimating the demand and cost conditions that determine equilibrium profit.

They then consider how structural commitments might alter those conditions and how competitors are likely to respond.

Through this process, expected profit can be evaluated before revenue exists.

Structural commitments influence the equilibrium environment.
Equilibrium competition determines the resulting profit.

Together they define the economic potential of the venture.

The Discipline of Anticipation

Entrepreneurial decisions often involve uncertainty about how markets will evolve.

The analysis presented here does not eliminate that uncertainty.
It does, however, impose discipline on how the future is considered.

Instead of focusing solely on visible price competition, firms examine the structural commitments that shape that competition.
Instead of assuming threats or advantages will succeed, they evaluate whether those commitments are credible and how rivals are likely to respond.

This disciplined anticipation allows profit to be estimated before the market exists.

Competition ultimately reveals the outcome.
Strategic commitment shapes the path that leads there.
