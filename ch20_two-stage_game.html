The Two-Stage Game

Structure first, price second

Competition rarely begins with price.

By the time prices begin to move, many of the conditions that determine the outcome have already been established. Firms have built infrastructure. They have chosen production technologies. They have designed products and positioned brands.
These decisions shape the environment in which price competition later occurs.
The sequence therefore matters.

Competition unfolds in two stages.
First, firms choose structure.
Second, given that structure, firms compete in price.

Understanding this sequence clarifies how profit should be evaluated before revenue exists.

Stage One: Choosing Structure

Structural choices are commitments that shape the economic environment of the market.
They are typically long-lived. Once made, they cannot be easily reversed.

Examples include:

  • The cost structure created by production technology or supply chain design.
  • The degree of product differentiation created through design and branding.
  • Distribution systems that expand or limit access to customers.
  • Infrastructure that determines scale or capacity.

These decisions determine the parameters that define the competitive landscape.

A lower marginal cost changes the economics of price competition.
Greater differentiation reduces substitution between rivals.
Expanded distribution shifts baseline demand.

None of these choices directly sets the final price. But each alters the conditions under which price competition will later take place.

Structural choices therefore shape the equilibrium that ultimately emerges.

Stage Two: Price Competition

Once structure is in place, firms compete in price.

At this stage, the parameters governing demand and cost are already determined. Firms adjust prices within those constraints.

The equilibrium price competition developed earlier applies here.

Given demand conditions and cost structure, equilibrium prices emerge. From those prices follow equilibrium quantities and profit.

Price competition is therefore the second stage of the competitive process.
It is the mechanism through which the consequences of earlier structural commitments are revealed.

Profit as a Two-Stage Outcome

This sequential structure allows profit to be written conceptually as:

\[ \textsf{Profit} = \textsf{equilibrium}(\textsf{Stage 2 pricing } | \textsf{ Stage 1 choices}) \]

In other words, equilibrium profit depends on the structural choices made before price competition begins.
If structural choices change demand parameters or cost conditions, they also change the equilibrium profit that price competition will produce.

The equilibrium calculations developed earlier therefore remain central. They simply occur one step later in the decision process.

Structural commitments determine the parameters.
Equilibrium price competition determines the resulting profit.

Anticipating the Outcome

Because firms understand this sequence, structural choices are not made blindly.

When deciding whether to invest in differentiation or reduce cost, a firm anticipates the price competition that will follow.

If a cost reduction leads to a more favorable equilibrium price and higher profit, the investment may be worthwhile.
If rivals are expected to respond in ways that eliminate the advantage, the same investment may produce little benefit.
Structural decisions therefore involve anticipating the equilibrium that will result after price competition unfolds.
Firms do not simply react to price competition. They choose structures that influence the outcome of that competition.

Reasoning from the End

The logic of the two-stage game can be understood by reasoning from the end of the process.

Start with the price competition that will eventually occur.

Given a particular set of demand and cost parameters, the equilibrium price, quantity, and profit can be computed. This is the second stage. Now move one step earlier.

Ask which structural commitments would produce those parameters.

If a firm chooses a cost structure or product design that alters the demand or cost environment, the equilibrium price competition will change accordingly.

Structural choices therefore determine which equilibrium eventually arises.

By starting with the equilibrium and reasoning backward to the structural commitments that create it, firms can evaluate whether those commitments are worthwhile.

Strategic Anticipation

In markets with competitors, this reasoning must also account for rival behavior.

Structural choices are rarely made in isolation.

If one firm invests in cost reduction, rivals may attempt similar improvements. If one firm differentiates its product, competitors may respond with their own designs. The structural environment that ultimately emerges reflects the combination of these decisions.

Nevertheless, the logic remains the same.

For any structural configuration, equilibrium price competition determines the resulting profit. By comparing the equilibrium outcomes associated with different structural configurations, firms can evaluate which commitments are likely to produce favorable results.

The purpose is not to predict every competitive move perfectly. It is to anticipate how structural commitments shape the equilibrium environment.

Why the Sequence Matters

Ignoring the sequential nature of competition can lead to misleading conclusions.

If price competition is analyzed without considering structural commitments, important determinants of equilibrium profit may appear fixed when they are not.

Conversely, structural investments may be evaluated without considering how they influence the price competition that follows.

The two-stage framework integrates these perspectives.

Structural commitments determine the parameters of competition.

Price competition determines how those parameters translate into profit.

Both stages are necessary for evaluating the economic potential of a venture.

Applying the Framework

The practical implication is straightforward.

When evaluating a proposed investment or business model, the analysis should proceed in two steps.

First, identify the structural commitments implied by the venture. These commitments determine the cost structure, differentiation, and market access that shape demand.
Second, compute the equilibrium that will arise once price competition occurs within that structure.

This process connects strategic design decisions directly to expected profit.

Structural choices are evaluated not in isolation, but through the equilibrium they create.

Structure Determines the Game

The distinction between the two stages clarifies a fundamental point about competition.

Price competition determines how firms divide the value created in a market.

Structural commitments determine how that value is created and how intense the price competition will be.

A firm that chooses a favorable structure may face less severe price competition. A firm that chooses poorly may find itself in a market where equilibrium margins are thin regardless of effort.

For this reason, the most consequential competitive decisions are often made before price competition begins.

Price determines the outcome.
Structure determines the game.
