Designing Advantage

Shifting the parameters of competition

Earlier chapters introduced the parameters that determine equilibrium outcomes in competitive markets.

Baseline demand determines how many consumers value the product at some price.

Price sensitivity determines how strongly demand responds to price changes.

Substitution captures how easily consumers shift between competing products.

Cost determines how much profit remains once production occurs.

These parameters—baseline demand (\(\mathsf{a}\)), price sensitivity (\(\mathsf{b}\)), substitution (\(\mathsf{d}\)), and cost (\(\mathsf{c}\))—define the competitive environment.

When these parameters are known, equilibrium price competition can be analyzed and profit estimated.

But firms do not simply inherit these parameters.
They often attempt to shape them.

Designing advantage means altering the parameters of competition in ways that shift equilibrium outcomes.

Baseline Demand \(\mathsf{(a)}\)

Baseline demand reflects the number of consumers willing to consider the product before price enters the decision.

A product with broad recognition, distribution, or relevance will typically face higher baseline demand than one that is difficult to access or poorly understood.

Firms attempt to increase baseline demand through several mechanisms.

Distribution systems expand market access.
Brand recognition increases consumer awareness.
Ecosystems or platforms attract users who value compatibility with other products.

These actions raise the initial level of demand that price competition later allocates.

Higher baseline demand does not eliminate competition. But it increases the potential scale of the market in which equilibrium occurs.

Price Sensitivity \(\mathsf{(b)}\)

Price sensitivity determines how strongly demand responds to price changes.

When consumers are highly price sensitive, small price differences cause large shifts in quantity demanded. When sensitivity is low, price changes produce smaller effects. Firms attempt to reduce price sensitivity by increasing the perceived value of their offering.

Product design, service quality, reliability, and brand reputation can all influence how consumers respond to price.

When consumers view two alternatives as functionally similar, price becomes the dominant decision variable. When the alternatives differ in ways consumers care about, price sensitivity declines.

In equilibrium, reduced price sensitivity often translates into higher sustainable margins.

Substitution \(\mathsf{(d)}\)

Substitution measures how closely competing products are viewed as alternatives.

When substitution is strong, consumers easily move from one product to another. Price competition becomes intense because demand shifts quickly toward the lower-priced option.

When substitution weakens, each firm retains a larger portion of demand even when prices differ.

Firms therefore invest heavily in differentiation.

Product features, design language, user experience, and brand identity can all alter the degree to which products are viewed as substitutes.

Differentiation does not eliminate competition. But it changes the structure of the demand system that governs price competition.

Lower substitution typically softens rivalry.

Cost \(\mathsf{(c)}\)

Cost determines the margin available once price competition occurs.

Lower marginal cost allows a firm to profit even when prices fall during competition. Higher cost reduces the range of prices at which profit is possible.

Firms therefore devote substantial effort to reducing cost through technology, operational efficiency, and supply chain design.

Automation, process innovation, and procurement relationships can all lower marginal cost.

Vertical integration may also alter cost structure by internalizing activities previously performed by suppliers.

When cost advantages are durable, they often produce persistent competitive advantages in equilibrium.

Strategic Moves That Shift Parameters

Several common strategic actions can influence these parameters.

Differentiation Investments

Investments in product features, design, or service aim primarily at substitution and price sensitivity.

A well-differentiated product is less easily replaced by competitors and may face weaker price sensitivity.

Such investments attempt to alter the demand relationships that determine equilibrium pricing.

Brand Architecture

Brand positioning and identity influence baseline demand and substitution.

Strong brands increase recognition and trust. They may attract consumers who would otherwise overlook the product.

They may also reduce substitution if consumers perceive the brand as offering distinct value.

Switching Cost Engineering

Firms sometimes create structures that make it costly for consumers to switch to competing products.

Compatibility with complementary goods, accumulated user data, or learning effects can all create switching costs.

These mechanisms weaken substitution and reduce the sensitivity of demand to rival price changes.

Infrastructure Scale

Large-scale infrastructure can influence both baseline demand and cost.

Distribution networks increase access to consumers. Production scale may reduce marginal cost. Platform ecosystems may attract additional users whose participation raises baseline demand.

Infrastructure investments therefore affect both sides of the equilibrium calculation.

Vertical Integration

Integrating upstream or downstream activities may alter cost and demand conditions simultaneously.

Control over production inputs may reduce marginal cost. Control over distribution may expand baseline demand.

Integration can also limit the ability of rivals to access key inputs or distribution channels, altering competitive dynamics.

Durable and Temporary Advantages

Not all strategic moves produce durable advantages.

Some investments create benefits that competitors cannot easily replicate. Unique technologies, proprietary processes, or deeply embedded ecosystems may generate lasting shifts in cost or demand.
Other moves are quickly imitated.

When rivals can replicate the same differentiation or cost improvements, the advantage disappears as the industry adjusts.
In such cases, the equilibrium parameters eventually return to a symmetric configuration.

The distinction between durable and temporary advantages is therefore central to evaluating strategic commitments.

Symmetric Escalation

Some strategic moves invite symmetric responses from competitors.

When one firm invests in cost reduction, rivals may attempt the same improvement. When one firm expands capacity or infrastructure, others may respond with similar investments.

The result may be an escalation in structural commitments without a lasting change in relative advantage.

In equilibrium, all firms may incur higher costs or greater investment while earning similar profits as before.

Such escalation increases the scale of competition without necessarily improving outcomes.

Recognizing this possibility is important when evaluating structural commitments.

Dynamic Rivalry

These dynamics illustrate how rivalry evolves over time.

Firms attempt to shift the parameters governing demand and cost. Competitors observe these moves and respond. The resulting adjustments reshape the equilibrium environment in which price competition occurs. Competition therefore extends beyond price adjustments.

It includes the strategic design of products, infrastructure, and organizational structure that determine the parameters of competition itself.

Understanding this process does not require complex mathematics.

It requires recognizing that equilibrium profit depends on parameters that firms actively attempt to influence.

Evaluating Structural Moves

When considering a structural commitment, the relevant question remains consistent with the earlier framework.

How does this move change the parameters of competition?
Does it increase baseline demand?
Does it reduce price sensitivity?
Does it weaken substitution?
Does it reduce cost?

If so, the equilibrium generated by price competition may produce higher profit.
But the analysis cannot stop there.

Competitor responses must also be considered. If rivals adopt similar strategies, the parameters may converge again and the advantage may disappear.

The purpose of the analysis is not to eliminate uncertainty. It is to clarify how structural decisions influence the equilibrium outcomes that determine profit.

Advantage as Parameter Design

Competitive advantage can therefore be understood as the deliberate shaping of the parameters that govern equilibrium competition.

A firm that successfully alters these parameters in its favor may face a more favorable equilibrium once price competition occurs.
A firm that fails to do so may compete within parameters that limit profit regardless of effort.

Strategic decisions are therefore not separate from economic analysis. They are attempts to redesign the environment in which equilibrium profit is determined.

Price competition reveals the outcome.
Parameter design determines the possibilities.
